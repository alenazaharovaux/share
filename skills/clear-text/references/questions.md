# The reader's questions

Every sentence and every paragraph is checked against these questions. If a reader would ask one of them, rewrite the spot so the answer is in the text. Each question has a rule, an example and where it came from: a quote of the skill author's comment, translated from Russian. Add new questions here after every round of comments.

## About a sentence

### 1. Who or what is this about?
A pointing word ("this, such, that, the other, the second, his, her, their") or a word like "the link", "the log", "the handbook" refers to something the reader has not seen or has already forgotten.
**Rule:** replace the pointing word with what it points to. After a long gap, name your own term in full: "the action — quote — reasoning link". Explain a code for a person or an object the first time it appears in each section: "r03, a mother of two".
**Example:** "The other question gives a different answer" → "The question 'Go back to the day you got this card' gives a different answer".
**Where it came from:** "Which other question? What was the first one?", "His — whose?", "Without context it is unclear who this R03 is" (article 07, November 2026).

### 2. What does this word mean?
A term, an abbreviation, an internal name, or a word that does not exist in the document being discussed.
**Rule:** explain a term in the same sentence where it first appears. Name a part of a document exactly as the document names it. Explain an internal name (handbook, HQ, skill): what file it is, where it lives, what it is for.
**Example:** "He does not take topics from the design in advance" → "The research design lists the research objectives (RO) and hypotheses (H); at this step the agent does not use them".
**Where it came from:** "Our research design has no 'Topics' section. What do we mean?", "What handbook is this? Where did it come from?".

### 3. Does the sentence make sense without its neighbours?
A short sentence that leans on the next one: "The step is needed for line numbers", "This way the model does not mix people up".
**Rule:** if the second sentence explains the first and the first makes no sense without it, merge them with a colon or a subordinate clause. Delete announcing phrases ("for a simple reason", "here is an example", "this step is needed because", "this matters") and write the thought itself.
**Example:** "The agent marks how it knows about the action. Either the respondent told it…" → "The agent marks how it knows about the action: the respondent described it, or the agent inferred it from other words".
**Where it came from:** "Why do we break things into these super-simple sentences? We lose the meaning", "What's wrong with a colon?".

### 4. What actually happens?
A pretty phrase or a metaphor: "looks for the common pattern and smooths out whatever sticks out", "makes the same mistake every time", "much harder to make up".
**Rule:** write who did what and what came out of it, with an example. A comparison ("harder", "better") names what it is compared with and the consequence.
**Example:** "looks for the common pattern and smooths it out" → "If several respondents did not come back because of slow delivery, the model will write 'customers do not return because of delivery' and lose r03, who stays for the loyalty points".
**Where it came from:** "Pretty but unclear phrase. What exactly do we mean?", "much harder — but possible, or what?".

### 5. How do we know?
A reference to a line, an article, a presentation or a study without the text itself; a claim about a system without checking it.
**Rule:** a reference to a line comes with the quote. A reference to an article comes with a link that opens it. Name a source only if the reader can open it or if the source changes how much to trust the claim. Write "the skill does X" only after checking the skill's file. If the text refers to lines of a document, attach the document.
**Example:** "High confidence: r03 talks about points in lines 14, 42, 48 and 50" → the same, plus the text of lines 42 and 48.
**Where it came from:** "Again no quotes for the lines, it's an empty claim", "We give the article title and a link to it. How so?", "An example from the presentation… do I even need to know it's from the presentation?", "Is that actually written in the skill?".

### 6. Where is it and where does it go?
An action verb without a place: "writes", "saves", "marks", "the log".
**Rule:** every such verb gets the place where the reader will find the result.
**Example:** "writes down what it fixed" → "writes the remark and the fix into the 'Check' column of the 'Findings' sheet in the Google spreadsheet".
**Where it came from:** "Where exactly does it write?", "Which log? … where should people look for it?".

### 7. Why?
A rule or a ban without a reason; a rating (confidence, type) without the signs it is based on.
**Rule:** explain the reason through the consequence for the result, with an example: what breaks without the rule. For a rating, list the signs.
**Example:** "One action per link" → "The three difficulties in line 26 will go into three different themes in synthesis. If they are recorded as one link, two of the three never reach the findings".
**Where it came from:** "It's unclear here why this is good", "how exactly does it form the confidence, what does it rely on", "why are such phrases allowed only in two cases. I think this is important".

### 8. Who acts, and in which system?
A sentence says "the model tends to…" when the text is about our own skill; mentions a prompt we do not have; describes a label without an action.
**Rule:** write about the system you are describing and only about what is in it. Phrase a label or a rule as an action: who does what.
**Example:** "'Needed from the observer' — what was on the screen" → "'Needed from the observer' means: the researcher asks the observer or checks the recording to see what was on the screen, and adds it to the analysis".
**Where it came from:** "Which model tends to throw things out? We're analysing skills", "Which synthesis prompt? Our HQ has methodologies", "An action is missing".

### 9. Is it too narrow?
A smooth phrase leaves one option where there are several, or oversimplifies a method.
**Rule:** list all real options. Describe a method with its caveats.
**Example:** "ask in a second wave of interviews" → "change the product, check the data, run a survey, a usability test or a second wave of interviews".
**Where it came from:** "and what if we need a survey, what if a usability test, what if we check analytics?", "I'm not sure this is just thematic analysis".

## About a paragraph and the whole text

### 10. Could you make a slide from this paragraph?
A paragraph lists more than three rules in a row, and nothing stays in the reader's head.
**Rule:** make a list or a table with an example for each item. Show lists of sheets, columns or files as a table or a tree with an example row.
**Where it came from:** "imagine I'm making a presentation slide from this paragraph", "I understand what is written here, but I can't take it in", "This needs an actual picture".

### 11. Where is the example?
A rule, a type or a category without an example.
**Rule:** each gets an example from one running case of the text.
**Where it came from:** "Examples of this would be good", "Without an example this is completely unclear".

### 12. Does the order add up?
Steps in the text go in a different order than in real life; two sources for the same thing; "sometimes" without a condition.
**Rule:** the order in the text is the order in real life. For two sources, say why each is needed. Replace "sometimes" with a condition.
**Where it came from:** "how did we already analyse something in the previous step, and now we're only preparing the transcript? I'm confused", "So which source is the real one?", "Why does the researcher sometimes write it and sometimes not?".

### 13. Does the text do its job?
A section does not serve the task of the text; the text ignores decisions made in the conversation; the ending sums nothing up.
**Rule:** write the task of the text in one phrase before writing and check every section against it. Before handing over, check the text against the decisions from the conversation. The ending sums up what the reader has learned.
**Where it came from:** "our task was to take apart this reverse-engineered skill… and here it's not even clear what we reverse-engineered", "you didn't account for the fact that we're adding HTML output to this skill", "better to write nothing than this… it didn't land".

### 14. What is the step called?
The step's name implies we first write badly.
**Rule:** call a check step a check: "clarity check of the wording".
**Where it came from:** "What does 'rewrite' mean? So we first write it unclearly and then clearly?".

### 15. Why was this built?
A project description says what it does and is silent about why it was needed when a ready-made solution exists.
**Rule:** for a home-made solution, say what the ready-made one lacked and what yours does beyond it. Take the reason from the project's documents; if you cannot find it, say so.
**Example:** "GitHub trends radar: a daily snapshot of repositories" → "the regular GitHub trends show what is popular today; the radar keeps watching every repository that has ever come into view and sees renewed interest, slow growth and fading".
**Where it came from:** "no, this description is incomplete. pull out of the project why GitHub Trends wasn't enough for me, why I needed my own radar".

### 16. Can you see it?
A story about a project, a website or a tool rests on one screenshot or on the words "no screenshot".
**Rule:** give every project 2–4 screenshots of different screens, each showing its own feature. Open a closed website in the user's browser, where they are already signed in. Leave out a screenshot only where the screen shows other people's personal data, and say why.
**Example:** "Class hub (no screenshot: the site is password-protected)" → four screens of the hub: "Now", the timetable, the calendar, announcements.
**Where it came from:** "make at least 2 screenshots for every project, better 4", "here's another project where one screenshot says nothing", "pull the screenshots, you have everything open in Google Chrome".

## Rules from earlier texts (before the skill)

- A sentence has a concrete subject: "an older person", "the central bank". If you announce a number, list the items right away: do not write "identifies four groups" without naming the groups.
- Delete filler phrases: "the practical takeaway", "it is important", "it is worth noting", "it follows that".
- Do not write "X, not Y", "not X but Y", "that is", "the thing is", "in fact".
- Write an interview finding as facts about named people: "Respondent A was saving for a flat. Respondent B paid a deposit on a new car".
- If a reader has to ask clarifying questions to understand what a sentence or paragraph is about, the text is bad and needs rewriting.
